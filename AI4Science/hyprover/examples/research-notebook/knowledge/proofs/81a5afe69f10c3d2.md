1. A critical-point stability observation.

Let f be a smooth function on a compact sphere with exactly two nondegenerate critical points. Every sufficiently small C^2 perturbation of f has exactly one nondegenerate critical point near each of these points and no others. We give details, including existence, to fix the global aspect of this assertion.

Choose disjoint coordinate balls around the critical points, with each critical point represented by 0. Write H for the invertible coordinate Hessian of f at 0. By shrinking the ball, arrange
||H^(-1)(D^2 f(x)-H)||<1/4
throughout its closure, which is a convex Euclidean closed ball. If g is sufficiently C^2 close to f, then
||H^(-1)(D^2 g(x)-H)||<1/2
on that ball and ||H^(-1)Dg(0)|| is as small as desired. For a ball of radius r, choose the latter norm below r/4. The map
T_g(x)=x-H^(-1)Dg(x)
then has derivative norm less than 1/2, and satisfies
|T_g(x)|<=|T_g(0)|+(1/2)|x|<3r/4
when |x|<=r. Its iterates are Cauchy because successive distances decrease by a factor at most 1/2; the closed ball is complete, so it has a fixed point. The same contraction estimate makes that fixed point unique. It is a critical point of g and lies in the interior. The inequality for D^2g makes its Hessian invertible. Outside the interiors of the two chosen balls, |grad f| has a strictly positive minimum, because that complement is compact and contains no critical point. Sufficient C^1 closeness therefore excludes any additional critical point. This proves the observation. If f and g are odd, their critical points occur in antipodal pairs, so exactly two of them form one such pair. All coordinate C^2 bounds used here follow from the usual C^2 sphere norm on fixed compact coordinate balls.

2. Exact centering for the coefficient map.

Put p=(n-2)/2, b_k=b_{n,k}, and define the scalar integrals
c_k=integral_{-1}^1 z^(2k)(1-z^2)^(p+1/2)dz,
d_k=integral_{-1}^1 (1-t^2)^(k+p+1/2)dt.
Integrate the nonnegative continuous function
z^(2k)(1-z^2-t^2)^p
over the disk z^2+t^2<=1. At fixed t, substitute z=sqrt(1-t^2)q. The integral is b_k d_k. At fixed z, substitute t=sqrt(1-z^2)q. The same integral is b_0 c_k. Thus
b_k d_k=b_0 c_k
for every k. Positivity of b_k follows because its integrand is positive on a nonempty open subinterval of (-1,1).

Using the definition of v and the identity just proved gives
integral_{-1}^1 v(z)w_n(z)dz
 = (2 kappa_{n-2})^(-1) sum_k p_k c_k/b_k
 = (2 kappa_{n-2} b_0)^(-1) sum_k p_k d_k
 = (2 kappa_{n-2} b_0)^(-1)
   integral_{-1}^1 P(1-t^2)w_n(t)dt
 =0.
The last equality is precisely the two assumed weighted moment equalities, with all remaining vector coordinates identically zero.

The map (w,z) -> (w+epsilon v(z),z) is a smooth global diffeomorphism of R^n with Jacobian determinant 1 and inverse (w,z) -> (w-epsilon v(z),z). It maps the closed unit ball onto K_epsilon. In particular K_epsilon is compact, has nonempty interior, and has volume kappa_n. At height z its fiber is a ball of radius sqrt(1-z^2) centered at epsilon v(z). Therefore
integral_{K_epsilon} w dx
 =epsilon kappa_{n-1} integral_{-1}^1 v(z)w_n(z)dz=0,
and
integral_{K_epsilon} z dx
 =kappa_{n-1} integral_{-1}^1 z w_n(z)dz=0.
The latter integrand is odd. Hence c(K_epsilon)=0 exactly, for every epsilon for which the body is considered; no translation or asymptotic centering is involved.

3. Smoothness and strict convexity for small epsilon.

Let
Phi_epsilon(w,z)=|w-epsilon v(z)|^2+z^2.
Its level set Phi_epsilon=1 is smooth. Indeed if its w-gradient vanishes, then w=epsilon v(z), and the level equation forces z=+1 or -1; its z-derivative is then 2z, which is nonzero. At every other point the w-gradient itself is nonzero. Smoothness also follows from the global shear diffeomorphism of the unit sphere.

To prove strict convexity, let V=max_{|z|<=1}|v(z)| and W=max_{|z|<=1}|v''(z)|. For |epsilon|<=1 the whole set K_epsilon lies in the convex cylinder
C={|z|<=1, |w|<=1+V}.
For any vector (h,s) its second directional derivative is
D^2 Phi_epsilon(w,z)[(h,s),(h,s)]
 =2|h-epsilon v'(z)s|^2
  +2(1-epsilon (w-epsilon v(z)) dot v''(z))s^2.
On C we have |w-epsilon v(z)|<=1+2V. Choose |epsilon| sufficiently small that
|epsilon|(1+2V)W<1/2.
Then the displayed second derivative is at least
2|h-epsilon v'(z)s|^2+s^2,
which is positive for every nonzero (h,s). For distinct x,y in K_epsilon their joining segment lies in C. Restricting Phi_epsilon to that segment and integrating its strictly positive second derivative yields
Phi_epsilon((1-lambda)x+lambda y)
 <(1-lambda)Phi_epsilon(x)+lambda Phi_epsilon(y)<=1
for every 0<lambda<1. The strict sublevel set is open, so these interior segment points lie in int(K_epsilon). This proves convexity and the specified strict convexity. Also Phi_epsilon(0)=epsilon^2|v(0)|^2<1 for sufficiently small epsilon, so the origin is interior. These properties include epsilon=0 where appropriate.

4. Smooth dependence of the signed-volume function.

For all sufficiently small epsilon, write rho_epsilon(theta) for the radial function of K_epsilon about 0. We justify its smooth dependence rather than differentiating a discontinuous kernel. The equation
Phi_epsilon(r theta)=1
has r=1 when epsilon=0; its derivative in r there is 2, for every theta on the sphere. The elementary smooth implicit-function theorem and compactness of the sphere give a positive C-infinity function rho_epsilon(theta), smooth jointly in theta and epsilon near epsilon=0. For small epsilon this is the actual boundary radius: 0 is interior and strict convexity makes every ray meet the boundary in exactly one positive point. The local implicit functions therefore agree wherever their domains overlap.

Polar integration gives
S_epsilon(u)=(1/n) integral_{S^{n-1}}
 sign(u dot theta) rho_epsilon(theta)^n d sigma(theta).
At epsilon=0 this is zero by the antipodal symmetry of the sphere. To see smoothness in u, take any small coordinate neighborhood on the sphere and a smooth choice of orthogonal matrix Q(u) satisfying Q(u)e_n=u. Such a choice can be obtained by projecting a fixed local frame onto u's perpendicular space and applying Gram-Schmidt near a fixed normal. Changing variables theta=Q(u)omega gives
S_epsilon(u)=(1/n) integral_{S^{n-1}}
 sign(omega_n) rho_epsilon(Q(u)omega)^n d sigma(omega).
Now the integration region and sign factor are fixed. Every derivative in u or epsilon falls on the smooth density. Its derivatives are bounded uniformly on compact parameter neighborhoods, so differentiation under the integral is justified by bounded domination. A finite cover shows that S_epsilon is smooth jointly in (epsilon,u), with uniform bounds for any fixed finite number of derivatives.

Consequently, if D(u)=partial_epsilon S_epsilon(u)|_{epsilon=0}, Taylor's integral remainder, differentiated in u up to order two, gives
||S_epsilon/epsilon-D||_{C^2}=O(|epsilon|).
Explicitly the difference equals
epsilon integral_0^1 (1-r) partial_epsilon^2 S_{r epsilon}(u) dr,
whose C^2 norm has the asserted bound by joint smoothness and compactness.

5. Computation of the first variation in every orientation.

Write a unit vector as u=(a,t), where a in R^(n-1), and put s=|a|. Suppose first that s>0. Change variables by the shear, so that integration is over the unit ball:
S_epsilon(u)=integral_{-1}^1 integral_{|w|<=sqrt(1-z^2)}
 sign(a dot w+t z+epsilon a dot v(z))dw dz.
Let xi be the coordinate of w in the direction a/s. The cross-sectional (n-2)-volume of the horizontal ball at xi is
kappa_{n-2}(1-z^2-xi^2)_+^p,
where r_+=max(r,0) and p=(n-2)/2>0. This density is continuous, including at the endpoints of its support. Differentiating the one-dimensional signed distribution in its threshold at epsilon=0 therefore gives
D(u)=2 kappa_{n-2} integral_{-1}^1
 ((a dot v(z))/s)(1-z^2-t^2 z^2/s^2)_+^p dz.
For completeness, the difference of the two sign functions is supported on an interval of xi of length |epsilon a dot v(z)|/s and has absolute value at most 2. The difference quotient is bounded by
2 kappa_{n-2}|a dot v(z)|/s,
an integrable function of z. The threshold derivative can thus be integrated in z by dominated convergence. This also covers thresholds at the support endpoints because the density there is zero.

Since s^2+t^2=1, the bracket is 1-z^2/s^2. Substitute z=s q on its support [-s,s]. The result is
D(u)=2 kappa_{n-2} a dot integral_{-1}^1
 v(s q)(1-q^2)^p dq.
Expanding v and using the definition of b_k yields
D(u)=a dot sum_{k=0}^N p_k s^(2k)
    =a dot P(1-t^2)
    =u_1 A(t^2)+u_2 B(t^2)
    =Psi(u).
At s=0, u=+e_n or -e_n, and S_epsilon(u)=0 for every epsilon because the z marginal of K_epsilon is symmetric. Thus D(u)=0=Psi(u) there as well. Alternatively the polynomial formula extends continuously to these two normals. This proves D=Psi globally and establishes the stated C^2 asymptotic formula.

By the stability observation in part 1, for all sufficiently small nonzero epsilon the function S_epsilon/epsilon has exactly two nondegenerate critical points. Multiplying by epsilon does not change its critical set or nondegeneracy. The function S_epsilon is odd in u because sign((-u) dot x)=-sign(u dot x) away from a null hyperplane, and sign(0)=0. Hence the two critical points are antipodal.

6. Critical points are precisely the centroid-matching sections.

Here is a direct differentiation proof of the section identity, valid for the bodies just constructed. More generally take a compact convex K with 0 in its interior. Fix u in S^{n-1} and h perpendicular to u. On the normal curve u(tau)=(u+tau h)/|u+tau h|, signs are unchanged by the positive normalization. Decompose x=y+r u with y in u-perp. For every fixed y the slice of K on the line y+R u is an interval, possibly empty. Thus
S_K(u(tau))-S_K(u)
 =integral_{u-perp} integral_{r: y+r u in K}
  [sign(r+tau h dot y)-sign(r)]dr dy.
If y lies in the relative interior of C=K intersect u-perp, then y is interior to K. To see this, use 0 in int K and choose a point y' in C further along the ray through y, so y=lambda y' with 0<=lambda<1. Convexity and a small ball around 0 show that y has a neighborhood in K. The inner interval then contains a neighborhood of r=0, and its derivative at tau=0 is 2 h dot y. If y is outside C, the compact inner interval is separated from 0, so its derivative is zero.

The boundary of C has (n-1)-dimensional measure zero. One proof uses 0 in int C: for 0<delta<1 its boundary lies in (1+delta)C minus (1-delta)int C. The difference of these two volumes tends to zero by the scaling law; the inclusion follows from convexity and the interior ball at 0. Thus only a null set of y is unaccounted for. The inner difference quotient is always bounded in absolute value by 2|h dot y|, because its support in r has length at most |tau h dot y|. The projection of K is compact. Dominated convergence therefore gives
d S_K(u)[h]=2 integral_{K intersect u-perp} h dot y dH^{n-1}(y).
The section moment lies in u-perp. It vanishes if and only if this derivative is zero for every tangent vector h. Every central section has positive (n-1)-measure, since K contains a ball around 0. Its moment vanishes if and only if its centroid is 0.

Finally, the centroid of a positive-measure affine hyperplane section belongs to its defining hyperplane, since that hyperplane is specified by an affine linear equation preserved by integration. Therefore a hyperplane section with centroid 0 must pass through 0. The two antipodal critical normals of S_epsilon consequently represent exactly one geometric affine hyperplane with the required centroid, and there are no others. This proves the entire conditional realization.

7. Producing the polynomial input in each dimension.

By predecessor 83fc8a6190cb2ca3, for this n there is a smooth even function alpha_n, equal to 0 near 0 and to 2*pi near |t|=1, such that
u_1 cos(alpha_n(u_3))+u_2 sin(alpha_n(u_3))
has zero vector first spherical moment and precisely the two nondegenerate critical points +e_1 and -e_1. Apply the orthogonal coordinate permutation exchanging coordinates 3 and n (the identity when n=3). This fixes e_1,e_2 and preserves sphere measure and critical-point nondegeneracy. It gives
f(u)=u_1 cos(alpha_n(u_n))+u_2 sin(alpha_n(u_n))
with the same properties.

Define smooth real functions on [0,1] by
a_*(r)=cos(alpha_n(sqrt(r))),
b_*(r)=sin(alpha_n(sqrt(r))).
They are smooth at r=0 because alpha_n is constant in a neighborhood of 0; they are smooth at r=1 because alpha_n is constant near 1. Away from 0 the square-root composition is smooth. Thus
f(u)=u_1 a_*(u_n^2)+u_2 b_*(u_n^2).

Let omega denote a point of S^{n-2} in the first n-1 coordinates. In the coordinates u=(sqrt(1-t^2)omega,t), the spherical area element is
(1-t^2)^((n-3)/2)d sigma_{n-2}(omega)dt.
This formula follows because the horizontal tangent directions scale by sqrt(1-t^2), in n-2 independent directions, while the t-direction tangent has length 1/sqrt(1-t^2). By coordinate reflection on S^{n-2}, the mixed coordinate integrals vanish. By coordinate permutations and sum omega_j^2=1,
integral_{S^{n-2}} omega_i^2 d sigma_{n-2}
 =|S^{n-2}|/(n-1).
Consequently
integral_{S^{n-1}} u_1 f(u)d sigma
 =(|S^{n-2}|/(n-1)) integral_{-1}^1 w_n(t)a_*(t^2)dt,
and the analogous formula for u_2 uses b_*. The zero first moment of f therefore gives both required weighted mean-zero identities for a_* and b_*.

We provide an elementary C^2 polynomial approximation argument. Every continuous real function h on [0,1] can be approximated uniformly by polynomials. Specifically, define
B_m h(r)=sum_{j=0}^m h(j/m) binom(m,j) r^j(1-r)^(m-j).
The weights are nonnegative, sum to 1, and have weighted mean r and weighted squared deviation of j/m from r equal to r(1-r)/m. These identities follow by differentiating the binomial identity (r+(1-r))^m=1 in two independent variables before substituting them. For any eta>0, uniform continuity makes |h(x)-h(r)| uniformly small when |x-r|<eta. The total weight of indices with |j/m-r|>=eta is at most r(1-r)/(m eta^2)<=1/(4m eta^2), by the squared-deviation identity. Thus
|B_m h(r)-h(r)|
 <=sup_{|x-y|<eta}|h(x)-h(y)|+2||h||_infinity/(4m eta^2).
First choose eta small, then m large; this proves uniform convergence.

For a C^2 function h, apply this uniform approximation to h''. If q is a polynomial with ||q-h''||_infinity<=delta, set
p_h(r)=h(0)+h'(0)r+integral_0^r(r-s)q(s)ds.
This is a polynomial, and integration gives
||p_h''-h''||_infinity<=delta,
||p_h'-h'||_infinity<=delta,
||p_h-h||_infinity<=delta/2.
Hence polynomials approximate h in C^2.

Choose polynomials A_0 and B_0 approximating a_* and b_* as closely as desired in C^2. Let
J=integral_{-1}^1 w_n(t)dt>0,
m_A=J^(-1) integral_{-1}^1 w_n(t)A_0(t^2)dt,
m_B=J^(-1) integral_{-1}^1 w_n(t)B_0(t^2)dt,
and set A=A_0-m_A, B=B_0-m_B. These are polynomials with the exact stated weighted zero means. Since a_* and b_* have those zero means already,
|m_A|<=||A_0-a_*||_infinity,
|m_B|<=||B_0-b_*||_infinity.
Their subtraction therefore still allows A,B to be arbitrarily close to a_*,b_* in C^2.

It follows that Psi(u)=u_1 A(u_n^2)+u_2 B(u_n^2) is arbitrarily close to f in C^2 on the sphere. Explicitly the ambient first and second derivatives of the difference involve only the two scalar coefficient errors and their first two derivatives, multiplied by bounded coordinates and fixed constants on |u|<=1. The spherical gradient is the orthogonal tangential projection of the ambient gradient; its Hessian on tangent vectors is the ambient Hessian minus (u dot grad_ambient) times the round metric. Those formulas show the required uniform C^2 bound. The observation in part 1 therefore supplies exactly two nondegenerate critical points when the approximation is sufficiently close. Oddness of Psi follows from its defining form.

This constructs admissible A,B in every n>=3. Applying parts 2-6 gives the asserted smooth strictly convex exactly centered examples in every such dimension, with the explicit polynomial-shear formula. It proves the high-dimensional existence conclusion without presupposing a smoothing procedure, a recentering argument, or invertibility of a spherical transform.
