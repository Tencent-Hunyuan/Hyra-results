We prove all assertions without any regularity assumption on the boundary.

1. The area centroid is an interior point.
First we establish c=c(K) in int K. We will use the following elementary consequence of nearest-point minimality. If y is outside the compact convex set K and p in K minimizes |y-p|, then
(y-p) dot (x-p) <= 0 for every x in K.
Indeed, for 0<t<=1 the point p+t(x-p) belongs to K, so
|y-p-t(x-p)|^2 >= |y-p|^2.
Subtracting the right-hand side, dividing by t, and letting t decrease to zero gives the inequality.

If c were outside K, apply this inequality with y=c and integrate in x. Since integral_K (x-p) dA=|K|(c-p), it would give |K||c-p|^2<=0, a contradiction. Thus c belongs to K.

If c were a boundary point, choose y_j outside K tending to c and corresponding closest points p_j. Because dist(y_j,K)<=|y_j-c|, we have |p_j-c|<=2|y_j-c|, so p_j tends to c. Along a subsequence the unit vectors n_j=(y_j-p_j)/|y_j-p_j| converge to a unit vector n. The preceding inequality divided by |y_j-p_j| and passed to the limit gives
n dot (x-c)<=0 for all x in K.
Choose an open disk B(a,r) contained in K, with r>0. Substituting a+t n for 0<t<r and taking t up to r gives n dot (a-c)<=-r. On B(a,r/2) we therefore have n dot (x-c)<=-r/2. Since this disk has positive area and n dot (x-c)<=0 on all of K, it follows that integral_K n dot (x-c) dA<0. The definition of c makes this integral zero, a contradiction. Hence c is interior.

Translation preserves all the centroid equalities and the number of section lines. We now translate K so c(K)=0.

2. A continuous radial function describes all relevant chords.
Choose r,R>0 with B(0,r) contained in K and K contained in the closed disk of radius R. For a unit vector u define
rho(u)=max{t>=0: t u belongs to K}.
Compactness and convexity give r<=rho(u)<=R and K intersect {t u:t>=0}={t u:0<=t<=rho(u)}. The function rho is continuous. To see this, suppose u_j tends to u. Any subsequential limit s of rho(u_j) satisfies s u in K by closedness, so s<=rho(u). Conversely, for 0<t<rho(u), put lambda=t/rho(u). Convexity and B(0,r) contained in K show
B(t u,(1-lambda)r) contained in K,
because t u=lambda rho(u)u and lambda rho(u)u+(1-lambda)B(0,r) is contained in K. Thus t u_j lies in K for all sufficiently large j, giving liminf rho(u_j)>=t. Letting t increase to rho(u), and using the upper bound from subsequential limits, proves continuity. This argument also proves that every point t u with 0<=t<rho(u) is interior.

Write u(theta)=(cos theta,sin theta) and rho(theta)=rho(u(theta)); rho is continuous and 2pi-periodic. The chord on the line R u(theta) is
[-rho(theta+pi)u(theta), rho(theta)u(theta)].
It has positive length, and integration along this interval gives its length centroid as
((rho(theta)-rho(theta+pi))/2)u(theta).
Consequently its centroid equals 0 exactly when rho(theta)=rho(theta+pi). Moreover every centroid-matching affine line must contain 0: if L has equation n dot x=b with n nonzero, integration along the section gives n dot c(K intersect L)=b. Thus our central chords account for every matching line, and theta and theta+pi specify the same geometric line.

Polar integration of the first moment gives
0=integral_K x dA=(1/3) integral_0^{2pi} rho(theta)^3 u(theta) dtheta.
Define
f(theta)=rho(theta)^3-rho(theta+pi)^3.
Then f is continuous and f(theta+pi)=-f(theta). Changing variable by pi in the second term shows
integral_0^{2pi} f(theta)u(theta) dtheta
=2 integral_0^{2pi} rho(theta)^3u(theta) dtheta=0.
In particular, both integral f(theta)cos(theta) dtheta and integral f(theta)sin(theta) dtheta over [0,2pi] vanish. Since rho is positive, a matching chord is exactly a zero of f.

3. Elementary zero-counting lemma.
We claim that any continuous function f on R satisfying f(theta+pi)=-f(theta) and
integral_0^{2pi} f(theta)cos(theta) dtheta
=integral_0^{2pi} f(theta)sin(theta) dtheta=0
has at least three distinct zero classes modulo pi.

Suppose otherwise, so its zeros have at most two classes modulo pi. In particular f is not identically zero. Antiperiodicity lets us choose a real a with f(a)>0, and then f(a+pi)<0. There is at least one zero in (a,a+pi), by continuity, and there are at most two. Neither endpoint is a zero. List the zeros in this interval in increasing order. On each complementary open interval the sign is constant, because f is continuous and nonzero there. The first sign is positive and the last negative. There are at most two opportunities to switch sign, and the number of switches must be odd. Thus there is exactly one sign-switching zero z. Any other zero preserves the signs on its two sides. Therefore
f(theta)>=0 for a<=theta<=z,
f(theta)<=0 for z<=theta<=a+pi.
Let h(theta)=-sin(theta-z). Since a<z<a+pi, h is positive on [a,z), negative on (z,a+pi], and zero at z. Hence f h>=0 throughout [a,a+pi], and f(a)h(a)>0. Continuity makes the integral of f h over that interval strictly positive. Both f and h are pi-antiperiodic, so their product is pi-periodic; consequently integral_0^{2pi} f h>0. But
h(theta)=sin(z)cos(theta)-cos(z)sin(theta),
so the assumed vanishing of the two first moments gives integral_0^{2pi} f h=0. This contradiction proves the lemma.

The proof covers tangential zeros through the sign-preserving alternative. If the zero set contains an interval or otherwise infinitely many classes, the required three classes already exist, and if f is identically zero every direction works. Applying the lemma to the f in Step 2 gives at least three distinct unoriented centroid-matching lines for K.

4. Triangles have exactly three matching lines.
Let T be the convex hull of three noncollinear points v_1,v_2,v_3. Its area centroid is (v_1+v_2+v_3)/3. For completeness, on the reference triangle
Delta={(s,t):s>=0,t>=0,s+t<=1},
the area is integral_0^1 (1-s) ds=1/2 and
integral_Delta s ds dt=integral_0^1 s(1-s) ds=1/6.
Interchanging s and t gives the same integral for t. The invertible affine map
(s,t) -> v_1+s(v_2-v_1)+t(v_3-v_1)
has constant nonzero absolute Jacobian, so these ratios show that the centroid of T is the stated average.

Translate T to have centroid 0, and keep the vertex notation v_i; thus v_1+v_2+v_3=0. For each x in R^2 let lambda_i(x), i=1,2,3, be its unique affine barycentric coordinates, so
lambda_1+lambda_2+lambda_3=1 and x=sum_i lambda_i(x)v_i.
The reference-triangle affine map shows that x belongs to T precisely when every lambda_i(x)>=0, and that x belongs to its boundary precisely when this holds and at least one coordinate is zero. Also lambda_i(0)=1/3. Since these coordinates are affine functions,
lambda_i(-x)=2/3-lambda_i(x).

A centroid-matching line has opposite endpoints x,-x on the boundary of T, with x nonzero, by Step 2. Conversely, if x and -x belong to the boundary, they are the two endpoints of the central chord in their direction: the interior-point conclusion for all t<rho in Step 2 rules out a boundary point strictly before the endpoint on either ray. Thus opposite boundary points characterize all matching lines.

For such an x, both x and -x lie in T, so 0<=lambda_i(x)<=2/3 for every i. Boundary membership of x gives a zero coordinate, and boundary membership of -x gives a coordinate equal to 2/3. These are different coordinates, so the remaining coordinate is 1/3. Hence (lambda_1(x),lambda_2(x),lambda_3(x)) is a permutation of (0,1/3,2/3). Conversely every such permutation gives x and -x on the boundary, so all six permutations produce exactly the possible endpoints.

If lambda_i(x)=0, lambda_j(x)=1/3 and lambda_k(x)=2/3, then
x=(v_j+2v_k)/3=(v_k-v_i)/3,
using v_1+v_2+v_3=0. Thus the six endpoints are the vectors plus or minus one third of the three side vectors. They yield the three lines through 0 parallel to the sides. These three unoriented directions are distinct, because two sides of a nondegenerate triangle cannot be parallel: they share a vertex, and parallelism would force all three vertices to be collinear. This proves exactly three matching lines for every nondegenerate triangle.

Step 3 gives mu(2)>=3, and the triangle in Step 4 gives mu(2)<=3. Therefore mu(2)=3.
