First recall the elementary centroid transformation identity, including for lower-dimensional sets. If E is a k-dimensional affine subspace, S is a bounded measurable subset of E of positive finite k-dimensional Euclidean measure, and T(z)=Az+b is injective on E, boundedness ensures the finite first moments needed for c(S) and c(T(S)). Its restriction to E multiplies k-dimensional measure by a constant J_E>0. In orthonormal coordinates on the direction space of E, this is the constant square root of the determinant of the Gram matrix of the columns of the restricted linear map. Changing variables gives integral_{T(S)}w dH^k(w)=J_E(A integral_S z dH^k(z)+b H^k(S)) and H^k(T(S))=J_E H^k(S). Their quotient is c(T(S))=T(c(S)). This argument also applies to an injective affine embedding from a lower-dimensional Euclidean space into a larger one.

For (1), Fubini's theorem gives zero first moment in the first d coordinates because integral_K x dx=0, and zero last-coordinate first moment because integral_{-h}^{h}t dt=0. Thus c(C)=0. If sup_K|a dot x|<=h, the section C intersect H_a is exactly the image of all of K under the linear embedding x -> (x,a dot x). The centroid transformation identity therefore gives c(C intersect H_a)=(c(K),a dot c(K))=0. The section has positive d-dimensional measure because the embedding is injective and K has positive d-dimensional volume. Compactness of K gives R<infinity, and nonempty interior gives R>0. If |a|<h/R then |a dot x|<=|a|R<h for every x in K, so this entire open ball of parameters is admissible. Distinct a give distinct graph hyperplanes, since equality of their graphs would imply a dot x=a' dot x for every x in R^d and hence a=a'.

For (2), L_f is compact because it is the continuous image of the compact set K times [-h,h] under (y,t)->(f(t)y,t). It has nonempty interior: since 0 is in the interior of K, K contains a small Euclidean ball about 0, and since f(0)>0 and f is continuous, f is bounded below by a positive constant on some interval about 0. The corresponding slices therefore contain a fixed ball times that interval.

To prove convexity, take (f(t_i)y_i,t_i) in L_f for i=1,2, and lambda in [0,1]. Put t=lambda t_1+(1-lambda)t_2 and s=lambda f(t_1)+(1-lambda)f(t_2). If s>0, the first coordinate of their convex combination belongs to sK, by convexity of K after division by s. If s=0, that first coordinate is 0. Concavity of f gives 0<=s<=f(t). Since 0 is in K and K is convex, sK is a subset of f(t)K for 0<=s<=f(t), with the zero case interpreted as {0}. Thus the convex combination lies in L_f.

The slice of L_f at height t is f(t)K. Its d-dimensional volume is f(t)^d |K| and its unnormalized first moment in R^d is f(t)^{d+1} integral_K y dy=0. Therefore the first d coordinates of the first moment of L_f vanish. Its last-coordinate first moment is |K| integral_{-h}^{h}t f(t)^d dt=0, since f is even. Its positive volume then gives c(L_f)=0. The section at t=0 is f(0)K times {0}, whose centroid is 0.

Now fix a unit vector u as in the statement and put K_u=K intersect u^perp. Because 0 is in the interior of K, K_u has positive (d-1)-dimensional volume in u^perp. Since scaling commutes with intersection with a linear subspace, the height-t slice of the vertical section L_f intersect (u^perp times R) is f(t)K_u. Within the Euclidean hyperplane u^perp times R, its measure splits as dH^{d-1} in u^perp times dt. Its unnormalized first moment in the u^perp directions is
(integral_{-h}^{h} f(t)^d dt)(integral_{K_u}y dH^{d-1}(y))=0,
using c(K_u)=0. Its last-coordinate first moment is
H^{d-1}(K_u) integral_{-h}^{h}t f(t)^{d-1}dt=0,
using evenness. The vertical section has positive measure because f is positive on the open interval and K_u has positive measure. Its centroid is therefore 0.

Any affine hyperplane section of K whose centroid is 0 lies in a hyperplane through 0: integration preserves the affine linear equation of that hyperplane. Hence all centroid-matching hyperplanes of K have the form u^perp. Distinct such hyperplanes give distinct vertical hyperplanes in R^{d+1}, none of which equals the horizontal hyperplane t=0. This proves the asserted q+1 lower bound.

For (3), an invertible affine T maps affine hyperplanes bijectively to affine hyperplanes and satisfies T(B intersect H)=T(B) intersect T(H). The centroid transformation identity at the beginning of the proof applies to both the full-dimensional body B and each positive-measure section. Thus c(B intersect H)=c(B) if and only if c(T(B) intersect T(H))=c(T(B)). Positivity of section measure is also preserved by its positive constant Jacobian. This proves the bijection and the final assertion.
