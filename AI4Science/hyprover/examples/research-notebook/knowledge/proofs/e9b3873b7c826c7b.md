All measures in this proof are ordinary Euclidean volume or surface measure. Only polynomial identities, changes to polar coordinates, and the divergence theorem will be used.

1. A spherical-average identity for homogeneous polynomials.
Put m=n-1>=2 and s=s_{n-2}=H^{m-1}(S^{m-1}). If R is a homogeneous polynomial of degree d>=2 in z in R^m, Euler's identity gives its outward normal derivative on S^{m-1} as partial_nu R=dR. The divergence theorem on the unit ball B^m therefore gives
integral_{B^m} Delta_z R dz=d integral_{S^{m-1}} R dS.
Since Delta_z R is homogeneous of degree d-2, polar coordinates also give
integral_{B^m} Delta_z R dz
=(integral_0^1 rho^{d+m-3} d rho) integral_{S^{m-1}} Delta_z R dS
=(d+m-2)^{-1} integral_{S^{m-1}} Delta_z R dS.
The radial integral converges because d+m-2>0. Combining these equalities yields
integral_{S^{m-1}} Delta_z R dS
=d(d+m-2) integral_{S^{m-1}} R dS.                                      (1)

2. The equatorial derivative average of an odd harmonic polynomial.
Fix j>=0, put k=2j+1, and let P be real, homogeneous of degree k and harmonic on R^n. Write x=(z,t) with z in R^m and t in R. Grouping monomials by their z-degree gives the unique expansion
P(z,t)=sum_{r=0}^{k} t^{k-r} P_r(z),
where P_r is homogeneous of degree r. In particular the constant polynomial P_0 equals P(e_n). Comparing the coefficient of t^{k-r-2} in Delta P=0 gives
Delta_z P_{r+2}=-(k-r)(k-r-1)P_r,             0<=r<=k-2.                 (2)
For 0<=ell<=j define
c_ell=s^{-1} integral_{S^{m-1}} P_{2ell}(omega) dS(omega).
Thus c_0=P(e_n). Apply (1) to R=P_{2ell+2} and integrate (2) with r=2ell. For 0<=ell<j this gives
(2ell+2)(2ell+m)c_{ell+1}=-(k-2ell)(k-2ell-1)c_ell.
Multiplying these recurrences gives
c_j=(-1)^j k! / [2^j j! product_{ell=0}^{j-1}(m+2ell)] P(e_n),          (3)
because product_{ell=0}^{j-1}(k-2ell)(k-2ell-1)=k!/(k-2j)!=k! and k-2j=1. For j=0 the product is empty, equal to 1, and (3) says c_0=P(e_n).

3. The hemisphere integral from a half-ball flux identity.
Let B_+={ (z,t): |z|^2+t^2<1, t>0 }, let S_+={ (z,t) in S^{n-1}:t>0 }, and let D={ (z,0):|z|<1 }. Apply the divergence theorem to grad P on the half-ball, whose two boundary pieces are S_+ and D; their common edge has zero boundary surface measure. Since Delta P=0, the outward derivative on S_+ equals kP by homogeneity and the outward derivative on D equals -partial_t P. Consequently
k integral_{S_+} P d sigma=integral_{|z|<1} partial_t P(z,0) dz.
In the polynomial expansion above, only the term with t-exponent 1 survives differentiation followed by t=0. Therefore partial_t P(z,0)=P_{k-1}(z)=P_{2j}(z). Polar coordinates in R^m give
integral_{|z|<1} P_{k-1}(z) dz
=(k+m-1)^{-1} integral_{S^{m-1}} P_{k-1}(omega) dS(omega)
=s c_j/(k+n-2).
Thus
integral_{S_+} P d sigma=s c_j/[k(k+n-2)].                              (4)

Let Y=P|_{S^{n-1}}. Odd homogeneity implies P(-theta)=-P(theta). Substitution theta=-eta on the lower hemisphere shows that its integral of P is minus the upper-hemisphere integral. The equator has sigma-measure zero. Hence
(HY)(e_n)=2 integral_{S_+}P d sigma
=2s c_j/[k(k+n-2)].
Insert (3), use k=2j+1, m=n-1 and k+n-2=m+2j, and cancel the factor k from k!. The result is
(HY)(e_n)
={2s(-1)^j(2j)!/[2^j j! product_{r=0}^{j}(n-1+2r)]} P(e_n)
=lambda_{n,2j+1}Y(e_n).                                                (5)
All factors in the denominator are strictly positive, and s>0; thus the displayed real scalar is nonzero for every j>=0 and n>=3.

4. Rotation gives the identity at every point.
For u in S^{n-1}, choose an orthogonal linear map R with Re_n=u and define P_R(x)=P(Rx). Orthogonal change of coordinates preserves the Euclidean Laplacian and homogeneity, so P_R is again harmonic and homogeneous of degree k. Since R preserves sphere surface measure and u dot R eta=e_n dot eta, a change of variables theta=R eta gives
(HY)(u)=integral_{S^{n-1}}sign(eta_n)P_R(eta)d sigma(eta).
Apply (5) to P_R to obtain (HY)(u)=lambda_{n,2j+1}P_R(e_n)=lambda_{n,2j+1}Y(u). This proves the asserted eigenvalue identity on the whole harmonic space, without any representation-theoretic or special-function assertion.

If k is even, P(-theta)=P(theta). The two terms in the integral defining HY cancel under theta -> -theta, because sign(u dot (-theta))=-sign(u dot theta) away from a measure-zero equator. Therefore HY=0 for even k.

5. Orthogonality, inversion and the first moment.
For real harmonic homogeneous polynomials P and Q of respective distinct degrees a and b, apply the divergence theorem on the unit ball B^n to the vector field P grad Q-Q grad P. Its divergence is P Delta Q-Q Delta P=0. Euler's identity on the unit sphere gives
0=integral_{S^{n-1}}(P partial_nu Q-Q partial_nu P)d sigma
=(b-a)integral_{S^{n-1}}P Q d sigma.
Thus restrictions of distinct degrees are orthogonal. In particular any finite sum of such spaces is a direct sum: taking its inner product with each component shows that a zero sum has only zero components.

If F is an odd finite harmonic sum, write it initially as a finite sum F=sum_k F_k with F_k in H_k. Since F_k(-theta)=(-1)^kF_k(theta), the relation F(-theta)=-F(theta) implies sum_{k even}2F_k=0, and the direct-sum property forces every even-degree component to vanish. The assumption of zero degree-one component therefore permits the expression F=sum_{j=1}^{J}F_{2j+1}. Define
g=sum_{j=1}^{J}lambda_{n,2j+1}^{-1}F_{2j+1}.
Every coefficient is well-defined and real. Each summand is the restriction of a real polynomial, so the finite sum is C-infinity on the sphere. Each degree is odd and at least three, proving oddness and the absence of a degree-one component. Linearity of the integral and the eigenvalue identity give Hg=F exactly.

For uniqueness within the real odd finite harmonic sums, if h is another such sum with Hh=F, then h-g is an odd finite harmonic sum whose transform is zero. Applying the nonzero eigenvalues and then the direct-sum property forces every component of h-g to vanish.

Finally each coordinate function theta_i is the restriction of a harmonic homogeneous polynomial of degree one. Its inner product with every summand of g is zero by the orthogonality just proved. Thus integral theta_i g(theta)d sigma(theta)=0 for every i=1,...,n, which is exactly the asserted vector first-moment identity. The empty-sum case F=g=0 obeys all conclusions.
